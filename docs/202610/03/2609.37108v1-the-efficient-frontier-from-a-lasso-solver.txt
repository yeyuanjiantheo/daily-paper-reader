# The Efficient Frontier from a LASSO Solver 

A practical guide to the CLA–LASSO identity 

Thomas Schmelzer 26 September 2026 

Abstract 

In a recent paper, Schmelzer and Hastie argue that Markowitz’s Critical Line Algorithm and the LASSO path trace the same curve. Here we use that identity to compute efficient frontiers with a stock LASSO solver, lars_path from scikit-learn . It handles long–short portfolios under a leverage cap, fixed leverage with varying risk appetite, and the classical long-only, fully invested frontier. Called naively, the last path stops at the maximum-Sharpe portfolio. One shift of the response, by an amount computed in advance, lets a single call reach the minimum-variance portfolio. On factor models with up to 500 assets every corner agrees with an independent quadratic program to within 10 −10 . On 494 S&P 500 stocks one call returns all 94 corners in 0.14 seconds, where a 100-point grid of QP solves takes about 12 seconds cold-started and 5 seconds warm-started. 

# 1 Introduction 

As the trade-off between expected return and variance moves, the optimal portfolio moves along straight lines and changes direction only at finitely many corner portfolios , where an asset enters or leaves. Markowitz’s Critical Line Algorithm (CLA) [13] walks from one corner to the next. The LASSO [19] behaves the same way as its penalty moves, and the LARS algorithm [3] walks its corners, which statisticians call knots. Schmelzer and Hastie [16] prove that after a change of variables the two paths coincide. This note is the practical side of that result, in three settings: 1. long–short under a gross-exposure cap, where the plain LASSO path is the frontier (Section 5); 2. fixed leverage with varying risk appetite, where the same path is rescaled (Section 6); 3. long only and fully invested, where the nonnegative LASSO path, shifted once, covers the whole frontier in one call (Section 7, Algorithm 1). The usual alternative is to solve a quadratic program on a grid of tilts. Each solve is only as accurate as its tolerance, and every corner between two grid points is missed. A path algorithm returns the corners themselves, and every other frontier portfolio is a linear interpolation between two of them. 

# 2 Background 

Markowitz [12] called a portfolio efficient if no other has a higher expected return E without a higher variance V , or a lower V without a lower E. Traced by minimising 12 V − λE over fully invested portfolios for λ ≥ 0, they form the efficient frontier, with the minimum-variance portfolio at λ = 0. The CLA followed in 1956 [13]. Tobin [20] and Sharpe [17] moved the picture to the ( σ, E ) plane, where Sharpe located the dominant portfolio, the one with the highest Sharpe ratio, at the tangent from the riskless rate. 1arXiv:2609.37108v1 [q-fin.CP] 29 Sep 2026 The LASSO fits least squares under ∥β∥1 ≤ t and so shrinks and selects at once. Its path is piecewise linear in t. Osborne, Presnell and Turlach [14] gave a homotopy for it and Efron et al. [3] gave LARS, which also covers the positive LASSO. Later practice moved to coordinate descent on a grid of penalties [5], which, like a grid of tilts, returns points rather than corners. The two literatures developed for decades without citing each other [16, §5]. The best-known bridge, Brodie et al. [2], read a Markowitz problem with a gross-exposure penalty as a LASSO, but swept the penalty at a fixed target return rather than tracing the frontier. Fan, Zhang and Yu [4] ran LARS along the gross-exposure cap of the minimum-variance portfolio. They removed the budget by regressing on a reference asset, which leaves an ℓ1 constraint that is no longer the LASSO’s, so their path is an approximation.

# 4 From mean–variance to least squares 

Let Σ ≻ 0 be the covariance of n assets and μ their expected returns. With the Cholesky factor Σ = LL ⊤, set X = L⊤ and y = L−1μ, so that X⊤X = Σ and X⊤y = μ. Then for every portfolio 

w 12 w⊤Σw − μ⊤w = 12 ∥y − Xw ∥22 − 12 ∥y∥22, (1) and under any constraints the mean–variance problem is a least-squares problem.    

> X = np.linalg.cholesky(Sigma, upper=True) # X.T @ X == Sigma y = np.linalg.lstsq(X.T, mu)[0] # X.T @ y == mu w = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y)[0] # unconstrained optimum Sigma^{-1} mu

The last line solves the normal equations X⊤Xw = X⊤y, which are Σ w = μ. With constraints, the optimality conditions become normal equations restricted to the assets held, a linear system in the trade-off parameter. The weights stay linear until the holdings change, and those changes are the corners. This is a change of variables, not a statistical model: X has one row per asset, and nobody measured y [16, §4]. Only X⊤X and X⊤y matter, so any square root of Σ will do. If Σ is a sample covariance from a T × n return matrix R with T > n , the centred returns X = ( R − 1¯r⊤)/√T − 1 work, with 

y from lstsq . For a factor model, stacking diag( d)1/2 over diag( δ)1/2U ⊤ gives an ( n + k) × n

matrix with no factorisation at all, and y = (diag( d)−1/2μ, 0). Both reproduce the Cholesky path to rounding.

# 8 Numerical evidence 

Two kinds of evidence are reported. The first needs no second solver: each portfolio the path returns is checked against its own optimality conditions. For the cap with multiplier κ these are ( μ − Σw)j = κ sign( wj ) on the support and |(μ − Σw)j | ≤ κ off it; for the long-only problem at tilt λ they are (Σ w − λμ )j = γ on the support and ≥ γ off it, with 1⊤w = 1 and w ≥ 0. The KKT residual is the worst violation, divided by ∥μ∥∞ or by the size of the gradient. The second compares with baselines that are independent of the path. The long–short problems go to Clarabel, an interior-point solver, through cvxpy ; the long-only problem goes to quadprog ,the active-set method of Goldfarb and Idnani [7]. 

Long–short. Every column has a KKT residual below 2 .6 × 10 −15 , and below 2 .9 × 10 −15 

once rescaled to fixed leverage. At each column’s cap Clarabel matches the path to 3 .0 × 10 −7

at the corners and 1 .4 × 10 −11 inside segments. The gap at the corners is the baseline’s, since interior-point methods are least accurate where the holdings change. At fixed leverage the figures are 6 .2 × 10 −6 and 1 .9 × 10 −11 .

# 9 When to use it 

The path beats a grid of QP solves, cold- or warm-started, on both speed and accuracy. LARS is in R and scikit-learn , the positive LASSO only in scikit-learn , so corner portfolios need no portfolio software, and one call shows that the knots of a LASSO path and the turning points of a frontier are the same points. It is the wrong tool when Σ is singular, where the identity is not known to survive [16, §7], or when the portfolio needs more than one linear constraint. The LASSO that scikit-learn solves has exactly one besides the signs, ∥β∥1 ≤ t:the gross-exposure cap of Sections 5 and 6, or, with positive=True , the budget 1⊤w = 1 of Section 7. The signs are either all free or all nonnegative. A dollar-neutral book adds the equality 

1⊤β = 0, and a 130 /30 book needs the budget and the gross cap at once. Path algorithms for a LASSO with general linear constraints exist [6, 9], but not in scikit-learn . A risk budget 

w⊤Σw ≤ σ2 has no path of its own, but along each segment the variance is quadratic in the parameter, so the portfolio at a given σ comes from one scalar equation. The numbers above test the implementation. Theorem 1 itself is proved in [16], and this note adds what a practitioner needs to use it: the shift, a ν0 computed in advance, and a max_iter 

large enough for big problems.
